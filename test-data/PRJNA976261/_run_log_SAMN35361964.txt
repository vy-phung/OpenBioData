Collection and storage of subgingival plaque samples\nFor each eligible subject, 4 subgingival plaque samples were col-\nlected at 4 teeth. In T2D + P−and T2D −P−subjects, sampling was\nalways performed at 4 sites randomly selected among those negative to\nbleeding on probing. In T2D + P+ and T2D −P+ subjects, sampling\nwas performed at the 4 sites showing the deepest probing depth values\namong those positive to bleeding on probing.\nAt each site selected for plaque sampling, saliva was blotted with a\ngauze or cotton roll, and supragingival plaque was removed with a\ncurette or scaler. Subgingival plaque was collected with a curette and\nwiped onto a sterile, coarse endodontic paper point. A commercially\navailable kit (OMR-110; DNA Genotek, Ottawa, ON, Canada) was used\nfor the storage of plaque samples. For each subject, the four samples\nwere stored together in a sterile microcentrifuge tube containing a\nﬁxative solution containing sodium dodecyl sulphate, glycine, N,N′-\ntrans-1,2-cyclohexanediylbis[N-(carboxymethyl)-, hydrate, and lithium\nchloride, at a temperature comprised between 15 °C and 25 °C, until lab\nprocessing for extraction of the oral microbiome DNA.\n2.4. Sample processing\n2.4.1. Isolation of DNA\nDNA was isolated from frozen samples by using the Maxwell RSC\nDNA Blood Kit (Promega) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.\nThe concentration of DNA was determined with the Qubit 2.0\nFluorometer (Life Technologies) by using the Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit\n(Life Technologies). Prior to library preparation, DNA was fragmented\nusing NEBNext® dsDNA Fragmentase for 30 min.\n2.4.2. Library preparation and sequencing\nEach library was generated from 20 ng of genomic DNA, in ac-\ncordance with procedures described in the NEBNext® Ultra™II DNA\nLibrary Prep Kit for Illumina protocol (New England Biolabs). NEBNext®\nMultiplex Oligos for Illumina® (Dual Index Primers set 1) were used to\nproduce the individual libraries and label them with speciﬁc molecular\nbarcodes. Then, Agencourt AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter) were\nused for library puriﬁcation. Finally, libraries were quantiﬁed by using\nthe High Sensitivity DNA Kit (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA,\nUSA) on the Bioanalyzer instrument (Agilent), diluted and pooled to-\ngether in equimolar amounts. Samples were sequenced with an Illumina\nNextSeq 500 sequencer with 2 × 150-bp read, using NextSeq® 500/550\nMid Output Kit v2.\n2.5. Bioinformatic analyses\nThe Illumina BaseSpace cloud platform (https://basespace.illumina.\ncom/) generated FASTQ ﬁles that were used for the analysis of the\nshotgun fragments. Sequence quality was ensured by trimming the\nreads using Trimmomatic 0.36 (Bolger, Lohse, & Usadel, 2014), with\nthe following parameters: CROP:147, HEADCROP:3, SLIDINGWIN-\nDOW:5:20, MINLEN:100.\nAll paired reads were aligned to the reference human genome\n(GRCh37-Hg19), using Bwa 0.7.15 (with option mem and parameters-\nM-t 20) (Li, 2013). Sequences displaying a concordant alignment (a\nmate pair that aligns with the expected relative mate orientation and\nwith the expected range of distances between mates) against the human\ngenomes and all orphan reads were then removed from all subsequent\nanalyses using SAMtools (Li et al., 2009) and awk custom ﬁlter.\nThen, BLASTN 2.6.0 (Altschul, Gish, Miller, Myers, & Lipman, 1990;\nCamacho, 2013; Li et al., 2009) was applied for aligning the remaining\nnon-human reads to the NCBI (NR/)NT reference database. The align-\nments with an e-value lower than 1 × 10 −6 were further ﬁltered to\nexclude matches with percentage of identity lower or equal than 95%\nand length lower than 100 bp.\nFinally, MEGAN6 (Huson, Auch, Qi, & Schuster, 2007) with default\nparameters was used to perform the taxonomical analysis of the data,\nranked for genus and species.\n2.6. Statistical analysis\nDue to the lack of previous studies with similar experimental design\nusing whole metagenomic shotgun sequencing and the absence of a true\nprimary outcome to determine an expected inter-group diﬀerence, no\nsample size calculation could be performed a priori. Therefore, the size\nof each group arbitrarily determined. The patient was regarded as the\nstatistical unit. Analyses were performed using STAMP v2.1.3 (Parks,\nTyson, Hugenholtz, & Beiko, 2014). Data were expressed as relative\nabundance of each taxonomic unit at either the bacterial genus or\nspecies level. Relative abundance was generated by normalizing the\nwhole genome sequencing data of each individual over the subject with\nthe lowest number of reads (IND5). To test the null hypothesis, White’s\nnon-parametric t-test (White, Nagarajan, & Pop, 2009) and Kruskal–-\nWallis H-test plus post-hoc tests (e.g., Tukey–Kramer) were applied to\ncompare proﬁles organized into two groups or multiple groups of\nproﬁles, respectively. To be more conservative, in the results section\nonly comparisons (both for genera and species) with a nominal p-value\nlower than 0.01 will be discussed. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure\nwas applied to control for the false discovery rate. Alpha- and beta-\ndiversity were used to describe the microbiome diversity within and\nbetween groups. Alpha-diversity (within-sample diversity) was mea-\nsured with the Shannon H’ diversity index, Pielou evenness J index and\nthe Margalef d richness index. Beta-diversity within sample (intra-beta\ndiversity), was evaluated with the Dβ index (as a component of total\ncommunity diversity, Dγ) while beta-diversity between individuals\n(inter-beta diversity) with the Bray-Curtis distance (Bray & Curtis,\n1957) and visualized with principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). For a\nreview about diversity indexes used, see Chiarucci, Bacaro, and\nScheiner (2011), Lozupone and Knight (2008) and Tuomisto (2010).\n3. Results\n3.1. Discussion\nSeveral studies have been published since the Human Microbiome\nProject in 2009, and whole metagenomic shotgun sequencing has been\nincreasingly recognized as the most comprehensive and robust method\nfor metagenomics research (Ng & Kirkness, 2010; Noecker, McNally,\nEng, & Borenstein, 2017). The application of whole metagenomic\nshotgun sequencing provides a detailed knowledge of species diversity\nand, in the most recent years, has allowed for revealing the true com-\nplexity of the dental bioﬁlm in either periodontally healthy or diseased\nconditions. Overall, our results seem to conﬁrm that period-\nontitis is due to a dysbiosis in the subgingival bacterial community\nresulting from the disruption of the host-microbial homeostasis by few\nbacterial species (Hajishengallis et al., 2011), rather than a true shift\nfrom some species to others.\nThe inclusion of type 2 diabetics in the study was subordinated to a\npoorly controlled diabetic status (as evaluated in terms of glycated\nhaemoglobin). Several studies have pointed out the role of hypergly-\ncaemia in determining an increased risk and severity of periodontitis\n(Cianciola, Park, Bruck, Mosovich, & Genco, 1982; Emrich, Shlossman,\n& Genco, 1991; Morita et al., 2012) and, in particular, type 2 diabetes\nshows a strong association with periodontitis incidence and severity\nwhen it is poorly controlled. (Chen et al., 2010; Khanuja, Narula,\nRajput, Sharma, & Tewari, 2017; Kowall et al., 2015; Novaes, Gutierrez,\n& Novaes, 1996; Tsai, Hayes, & Taylor, 2002). Interestingly, recent\nstudies using quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction or 16S rDNA-\nbased microbial proﬁling suggested that this relationship may reside in\nsome alterations of the subgingival microbiota that are dependent on\nthe level of metabolic control of diabetes. Miranda et al. (2017) ob-\nserved that the frequencies and counts of some periodontal pathogens\nwere increased in poorly controlled type 2 diabetes compared to well\ncontrolled type 2 diabetes. Also, Longo et al. (2018) reported diﬀerent\nlevels of ecological diversity in the subgingival microbiome of type 2\ndiabetics with adequate or poor metabolic control, with poorly con-\ntrolled type 2 diabetes showing increased levels of succinate/acetate\nproducers and decreased levels of butyrate producers. Overall, these\nﬁndings sustain the importance to characterize the subgingival micro-\nbiome of patients with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes with high-re-\nsolution techniques such as whole metagenomic shotgun sequencing.\nDiﬀerences in the subgingival microbiome between subjects with\nand without type 2 diabetes were observed for a limited number of\ngenera and species, only in presence of periodontitis and not in subjects\nwithout history of periodontitis. In particular, in presence of moderate-\nsevere periodontitis, poorly controlled type 2 diabetes was associated\nwith higher abundance of Dialister pneumosintes and lower abundance of\nFilifactor alocis and Treponema sp. OMZ 838 compared to systemically\nhealthy subjects. While Dialister pneumosintes and Filifactor alocis are\ntwo species with a well documented history of association with several\nforms of periodontal, peri-implant and endodontic infections, including\nperiodontitis (Aruni et al., 2015; Aruni, Chioma, & Fletcher, 2014;\nCollins et al., 2016; Colombo et al., 2009; Contreras et al., 2000;\nFerraro, Gornic, Barbosa, Peixoto, & Colombo, 2007; Gogeneni et al.,\n2015; Gonçalves et al., 2016; Silva-Boghossian, Neves, Resende, &\nColombo, 2013; Perez-Chaparro et al., 2018), Treponema sp. OMZ 838\nhas been only recently associated with necrotizing ulcerative gingivitis\nlesions in an anecdotal report (Chan et al., 2014). These few diﬀer-\nences, however, were not maintained after FDR correction, thus in-\ndicating that type 2 diabetes is not robustly associated with substantial\nalterations of the subgingival microbiota, but rather determines mild\nchanges in the abundance of few species (which include keystone pa-\nthogens, i.e. such species that initiate the cascade leading to dysbiosis\nand clinical manifestation of disease). Anaerolineaceae bacterium oral taxon 439 is an oral anaerobe\nbacterium isolated from pure cultures of human oral samples, but little\nis known about its relationship with destructive periodontal disease\n(Dewhirst et al., 2010). The Anaerolineaceae bacterium oral taxon 439\nwas ﬁrstly described only recently (Vartoukian et al., 2016). The dif-\nﬁculties in the isolation of Anaerolineae bacterium HOT-439 in plaque\ncultures were proven to be dependent on the need for “helper” bacteria\n(e.g., Fusobacterium nucleatum) for its growth in vitro (Vartoukian et al.,\n2016). Inferences from the available genomic data for this Anaeroli-\nneaceae sp. suggest a niche-speciﬁc adaptation for survival in host en-\nvironments, with regard to the ability to evade host defence system and\nthe ability to scavenge material from lysed cells/tissue (Campbell et al.,\n2014). Anaerolineae bacterium HOT-439 represents the ﬁrst oral taxon\nfrom the Chloroﬂexi phylum that has been cultivated from plaque\nsamples obtained from deep periodontal pockets. While this strain was\nfrequently isolated (three over four samples) in the study by Vartoukian\net al. (2016), other studies based on molecular analyses suggested that\nthe Chloroﬂexi taxon is a low-abundance member of the human oral\ncavity (Campbell et al., 2014; Dewhirst et al., 2010), with a prevalence\nof 0.003% (HOMD). A possible explanation for this inconsistency could\ncome from the presence of base mismatches between the ‘universal’\nbacterial primers, commonly used in 16S rRNA sequencing, and the\nAnaerolineae bacterium oral taxon 439 speciﬁc gene sequence, that might\nresult in a molecular detection bias against this taxon (Camanocha &\nDewhirst, 2014) with the 16S approach.\nBased on whole metagenomic shotgun sequencing, our analyses\novercome some technical bias observed in 16S rDNA-based microbial\nproﬁling being more eﬀective in the detection of low-abundant taxon,\nsuch as HOT-439. Thus, our results consolidate the existing literature\n(Abusleme et al., 2013; Szafranski et al., 2015) that assign this Chlor-\noﬂexi\ntaxon\nto\nthe\n‘core’\nperiodontitis-associated\nmicrobiome\n(Abusleme et al., 2013) and suggests it as a potential biomarker for\nperiodontitis (Szafranski et al., 2015).\nIn conclusion, the results of the present study showed that: (i) the\npresence of type 2 diabetes and/or periodontitis were associated with a\ntendency of the subgingival microbiome to decrease in richness and\ndiversity; (ii) the presence of type 2 diabetes was not associated with\nsigniﬁcant diﬀerences in the relative abundance of one or more species\nin patients either with or without periodontitis; (iii) a signiﬁcantly\nhigher relative abundance of Anaerolineaceae bacterium oral taxon 439\nwas observed in periodontitis patients vs individuals without period-\nontitis, with the diﬀerence remaining signiﬁcant when the comparison\nwas restricted to patients with type 2 diabetes.\nConﬂict of interests\nThe authors declare they have no conﬂict of interests related to the\npresent study.\nAcknowledgements\nThe study was supported by the Research Centre for the Study of\nPeriodontal and Peri-implant Diseases, University of Ferrara, Italy, and\nby research grants of the University of Ferrara (Scapoli, FIR 2017;\nSabbioni, FAR 2016-17).\nReferences\nAbusleme, L., Dupuy, A.
